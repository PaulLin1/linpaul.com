*When we wandered the cartoon streets of nowhere, you held me up and walked me home. Your youthful nature said, “Let’s call each other rockstars.” You said, “I’ll be what you want me to be. Call me what you want, call me Swiss Army Man.”*

The album begins with Doug Yule. His timbre is plain as vanilla, his melodies sweet as sugar. In _Who Loves the Sun_, he carries the hopeless naivety of a male in his early twenties. Perhaps Lou Reed had already been exposed to the soft white underbelly of society and couldn’t bring himself to sing songbook pieces as he once did during his teens or maybe simply because taking control of the band’s creative direction, made him focus more on songwriting and production. In any case, Yule’s voice carries listeners into the Velvet Underground’s prophesied eulogy. Luckily, Yule had a similar voice, allowing him to mimic Reed gentle side, with a tone similar to that of Reed's in _Sunday Morning_.

With the other two remaining Velvets, Moe Tucker and Sterling Morrison, on leave for personal reasons and Lou Reed honing his songwriting, Yule’s musical contributions outweighed those of all the band’s original members on this album. Yule took on the role of a multi-instrumentalist, playing every instrument on the album. With his natural melodic sense, he provided the bass playing that fit the professional-amateur sound the Velvet Underground had developed. However, to a trained ear, Yule’s performances are vastly different from and arguably subpar to those of the original members. Much of Yule’s legacy as a member of the band has been overshadowed by speculation of what the album would have sounded like if Moe Tucker had been on drums, if Sterling Morrison had played more, or even if Lou Reed had sung on all the songs. The charm of the Velvet Underground as a cohesive band crumbled as Doug Yule came in to become a jack of all trades.

The album ends with Doug Yule, this time with a voice too calm and airy to talk about socioeconomic issues. You begin to realize why bands are bands and why authenticity and craft are sought after by A&R men, and that John Cale’s classical background does mean he was a more important member than Moe Tucker, who had only just started learning drums when she joined the group. And only Lou Reed can sing about Lou Reed’s anger. An artist doesn’t bring harmonic perfection into the world through their craft. They bring experiences and interpretations unique to them. Perhaps Doug Yule did not have that.

*So, when tomorrow comes for you and you fiercely lash out, remember that you found a reason in 1970, and how what comes is better than what came before. Remember this life I breathed into you: you are only one of my creations.*
